If you’ve recently adopted a dog or are just looking to escape bad weather, an indoor dog park is an absolute lifesaver. Unlike traditional outdoor parks, indoor facilities offer climate control, sanitized flooring, and often coffee or drinks for the humans!

However, because these facilities are enclosed and space is more constrained, **etiquette matters more than ever**. Poor manners that might go unnoticed in a sprawling 5-acre outdoor field become immediate hazards in an indoor facility.

To ensure you and your dog have a safe, fun, and stress-free visit, we’ve put together the **Ultimate Indoor Dog Park Etiquette Checklist**. 

👉 **[Click here to view and print the visual checklist!](https://indoordogpark.org/tools/etiquette-checklist)**

---

## 1. Before You Go: Preparation is Key

A successful trip starts before you even leave your driveway.

### Check Vaccinations and Rules
Every indoor facility has strict entry requirements. Unlike public parks, indoor parks operate as private businesses and will turn you away if you don't have proof of current vaccinations (usually Rabies, DHPP, and Bordetella). Check if your dog needs to be spayed/neutered—most parks require this after 6 to 8 months of age.

### Burn Off the "Crazy" Energy
It sounds counterintuitive to walk your dog *before* going to the park, but it's a game-changer. If your dog enters the facility with 100% pent-up energy, they are likely to overwhelm other dogs at the gate. A 10-minute sniffari walk around the block takes the edge off and promotes calmer introductions.

### Leave the High-Value Items at Home
Do not bring your dog’s favorite ball, frisbee, or high-value treats into the play area. Resource guarding is the #1 cause of dog park scuffles. Keep treats in the lobby or your car.

## 2. Upon Arrival: Mastering the Lobby

The lobby of an indoor dog park is the most chaotic area. People are paying, dogs are entering, and excitement is peaking.

### Respect the Double Gates
Keep your dog on a short leash in the lobby. Wait your turn to enter the double-gated sally port (the transition area). **Never open the inner gate if the outer gate is open.** Once you are securely in the double-gated area, take off your dog's leash before opening the final gate to enter the play area. Entering on a leash can cause your dog to feel trapped and defensive when off-leash dogs rush to greet them.

### Remove Hazardous Collars
Before releasing your dog, remove prong, pinch, or choke collars. In a wrestling match, another dog's jaw or collar can easily get caught in a prong collar, leading to severe injury or panic.

## 3. Inside the Park: Active Supervision

The biggest mistake owners make is treating the dog park like a babysitter. 

### Put the Phone Away
Indoor parks are highly stimulating. You need to be watching your dog's body language 100% of the time. Are they getting overwhelmed? Are they bullying another dog? You can't intervene if you're scrolling on your phone.

### Keep Moving
Dogs are territorial creatures. If you stand in one spot or sit on one bench the entire time, your dog will likely "guard" that area and become defensive when other dogs approach. By slowly walking around the facility, you encourage your dog to explore and mingle neutrally.

### Clean Up Instantly
Indoor facilities are prone to smelling if accidents aren't addressed immediately. While staff are usually present with mops and enzymatic cleaners, it is *your* responsibility to pick up solid waste instantly. 

### The Consent Test
If your dog is wrestling with another dog and you aren't sure if it's mutual play or bullying, do a "consent test." Gently separate the dogs and hold yours back. If the other dog walks away, the play was one-sided. If the other dog eagerly bounces back to yours to continue playing, it's mutual!

## 4. Knowing When to Leave

Leave on a high note! The most common mistake new owners make is staying too long. 

A 45-minute visit is usually the sweet spot. Once dogs become overtired, they lose their manners and become cranky, which is when fights happen. If you notice your dog excessively panting, hiding behind your legs, or snapping at dogs they were previously playing nicely with—it's time to go home and enjoy a long nap.
